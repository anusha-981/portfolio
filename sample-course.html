Content Design Decisions

This tool was as much a content design problem as a product design problem. The language patterns evaluators see during a 4-hour review shift shape what they catch and what they miss. A few of the decisions that mattered most:

Three decisions that drove the tool's effectiveness

  • Label set. We tested several taxonomies before settling on three primary labels: “fabricated,” “partially supported,” and “unverifiable.” “Fabricated” replaced an earlier label of “wrong” because it forced evaluators to identify whether the AI invented the claim, which is the failure mode that matters for hallucination tracking.
  • Feedback microcopy. Early feedback was punitive (“Incorrect”) and pushed evaluator confidence down across long shifts. We rewrote feedback to be instructive (“This claim isn't in the source — flag as fabricated”), which kept evaluator decision quality steadier.
  • Pattern naming. The tool teaches evaluators to recognize four named hallucination patterns: invented citation, plausible-but-wrong number, character drift, and confident gap-filling. Naming them turned out to matter more than any single training session, because the names became shared shorthand on the team.

The result was a tool whose effectiveness was driven less by visual design and more by the precision of the language it used at every decision point. For a deeper walkthrough of how language design choices shape AI quality at the prompt level, see Designing System Instructions for Trustworthy AI.
