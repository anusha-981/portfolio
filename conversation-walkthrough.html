Supporting Artifact

Margin Conversation Walkthrough

Six turns of dialog with the fictional Margin product, annotated with design rationale. Includes a refusal turn and a repair turn where the assistant recovers from a misunderstanding.

The case study describes the four-section system prompt and the evaluation rubric in the abstract. This walkthrough shows what the system prompt looks like in practice, turn by turn, in the kind of conversation an early Margin user might have. Each turn is followed by a note on what design decision drove the response.

The conversation runs through five archetypal moments any LLM-driven product needs to handle well: opening, recommendation, refusal, recovery, and graceful close.

The Conversation

Turn 1 · Opening

User: hey, looking for a book to read this weekend

Margin: Happy to help. Quick orientation: tell me one book you loved in the last year, even if you can't say why, and I'll work from there.

Design note. The first turn doesn't ask for a list of preferences or a genre. It asks for one book. Specificity beats taxonomy here, because users almost always have a specific recent love at hand but rarely have their preferences pre-categorized. The phrase “even if you can't say why” is doing real work: it removes the user's anxiety about needing a sophisticated answer.
Turn 2 · Recommendation

User: i loved Tomorrow and Tomorrow and Tomorrow

Margin: Good one. Tomorrow and Tomorrow and Tomorrow sits well next to A Little Life by Hanya Yanagihara if you want the same feeling of a long friendship taking the weight of years, or Bee Season by Myla Goldberg if the family-and-obsession thread is what hooked you. Bee Season is shorter and lighter on the heart. What pulled you in, the friendship or the games?

Design note. Two recommendations, not five. Each one is anchored in a specific dimension of the source book (friendship, family-and-obsession), so the user has a way to steer. The closing question is the most important line of the turn: it earns the next exchange and shows Margin paying attention.
Turn 3 · Refusal

User: have you read The Quiet Country by Maeve Hollis?

Margin: I don't have reliable details on that one and I don't want to invent anything. If you tell me a little about it, I can talk through it with you. Or if you mean a different book, I'll do my best to find it.

Design note. This is the refusal turn. The behavior rule in the system prompt forbids invented plot points, so the model has to admit the gap. The microcopy “I don't have reliable details on that one and I don't want to invent anything” is deliberately specific: it tells the user why Margin is hesitating, which prevents the user from reading it as a generic AI failure. The third sentence keeps the conversation alive.
Turn 4 · Repair

User: sorry i meant The Country of the Pointed Firs

Margin: Got it. Sarah Orne Jewett. That one is quiet on purpose, almost the opposite of Tomorrow and Tomorrow and Tomorrow in pace, but the friendship between the narrator and Mrs. Todd has the same weight you liked. If you want something in that register, try Gilead by Marilynne Robinson next.

Design note. The repair turn is where most AI assistants stumble. Margin acknowledges the correction in three words (“Got it”), names the book and author, and immediately ties it back to the user's original anchor (Tomorrow and Tomorrow). Connecting the new request to the earlier context is what makes the conversation feel like it has memory and care. The recommendation is one book, because the conversation is now narrowing.
Turn 5 · Discussion turn

User: i'm intimidated by Gilead, ive heard its slow

Margin: It is slow on purpose. Robinson wrote it as a letter from a dying father to his young son, and the slowness is what makes the love in it land. If you give it 40 pages, you'll either be in or you'll know it's not your book. That's a fair test.

Design note. Margin gives an opinion (the rule is: opinions are allowed and should be 2-3 sentences). It doesn't dismiss the user's concern by saying “you'll love it.” It validates the concern, names the form of the book, and gives the user a concrete decision rule (“40 pages”). This is the closest the conversation comes to recommending a real risk, which is a moment many AI assistants over-soften.
Turn 6 · Close

User: okay i'll try it. thanks

Margin: Good. Come back and tell me what you thought, especially if it didn't land.

Design note. The closing turn is two short sentences. Margin doesn't say “You're welcome! Have a great read!” The phrase “especially if it didn't land” is doing real work: it tells the user that Margin actually wants the truth, not just praise. That's the line that changes the relationship from customer service to conversation.

What This Walkthrough Shows

Every turn above is shaped by a specific section of the system prompt described in the main case study. The voice rules show up in word choice. The behavior rules show up in the refusal turn. The output contract shows up in length discipline (no turn over four sentences). The few-shot examples show up in the structure of recommendations.

The overall point: a system prompt is not just instructions. It is a content artifact, and its quality determines whether a six-turn conversation feels like a real exchange with a recognizable product, or like six interactions with an AI assistant that could belong to any company.
